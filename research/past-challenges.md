### Levels 12 Startups in 12 Months: final scorecard

Started 1 March 2014, one launch a month, each given about a month to show demand or count as failed. The post's progress list shows 8 entries, not 12. On his project page (updated 25 Oct 2026): Success = Nomads.com (formerly Nomad List) and Remote OK; Okay = Go Fucking Do It; Failed = Play My Inbox, Tubelytics, Nomad Jobs (merged into Remote OK), GIF Book; #nomads is uncategorised. Remote OK is dated Feb 2015, so the year ran long. Tubelytics is dated 2013-2014 and his 2016 post says that build took a year, so one entry predates the challenge.

**What it means:** About 2 durable wins from 8 entries. Publish strict rules on what counts as a built app and report misses openly. Even the founding example of this format was loose about the count.

### Levels whole-portfolio hit rate

His page totals 114 entries: 9 Success, 11 Okay, 19 Failed, 69 non-profit or no profit goal, 6 new. Among the 39 with a commercial verdict that is about 23% durable (my calculation). The list includes non-app items such as merch and music nights. His legend defines Okay as took off and made money but not sustainable, and Failed as did not take off and/or did not make money. fly.pieter.com, the 2025 flight game, sits in Okay.

**What it means:** A serious operator with a large audience gets a durable win about 1 time in 4 or 5. Set the series' expectation at 1 or 2 survivors from 10, not 10 hits. A viral moment is not a verdict: his own taxonomy has a category for things that took off and still did not last.

- https://levels.io/projects

### How winners and failures looked different early

Levels wrote in May 2014 that his winners showed demand at launch: a few thousand sign-ups on day one from community posts, then a few hundred daily that kept growing. Failures had a traffic spike and nothing a week later, and some had taken months to build. His 2 or 3 winners took 1 to 2 weeks and were rough but useful. He also relays a Hacker News commenter's view that failing 90% of the time is fine if one in ten hits; that figure is a comment, not a measurement.

**What it means:** Measure each app again 7 days after launch, not just on launch day. The week-later number is the honest signal. Because 10 apps land in 10 days, the day-7 re-checks can become a follow-up episode.

- https://levels.io/run-through-ideas-quickly

### Nomad List origin: validate before building

On 24 June 2014 Levels tweeted a public Google spreadsheet asking people to help rank cities for nomads, and contributors added data and new criteria. The site went live 29 July, reached #1 on Product Hunt and Hacker News, drew about 50,000 uniques from HN and 12,000 from Product Hunt, gathered about 2,500 emails in two weeks and was profitable from day one through a sponsor. In September 2026 he reported about 12 years of operation and 43,252 paid members, and he made it nearly free. He told Lex Fridman it began when he had $500 a month to live on.

**What it means:** The strongest survivor began as a public artefact testing demand, not as code, and came from a problem he lived. For each app, spend the first hour on a public post, sheet or form aimed at the affected people. If nobody responds, swap the idea before building. It also fits the brief and makes a good on-camera moment.

### Compounding on one audience

Levels' two survivors share one community. Nomad Jobs merged into Remote OK, #nomads was folded into Nomad List, and Hoodmaps' early data and traction drew on existing Nomad List traffic. Yongfook reached a similar conclusion: his Tropic post prefers iterating over launching unrelated products.

**What it means:** My inference: with no audience, 10 apps for 10 unrelated groups means starting from zero 10 times. Grouping some apps around 2 or 3 communities lets one launch feed the next. This is the most useful structural change available for a creator with no audience.

### Play My Inbox debrief: fast build, quiet result

Built in one day. The April 2014 debrief reports over 2,000 users in the first month. Hacker News ignored it, and deleting and resubmitting under new titles got him hellbanned until a moderator stepped in. People were reluctant to grant inbox access, which he thinks limited spread. The largest user group, music journalists, was one he had not expected. On Lex Fridman in 2024 he recalled tens of thousands of users, well above his own 2014 figure.

**What it means:** Three takeaways: never delete and resubmit on Hacker News; explain any scary permission up front; and watch who actually shows up, because that may be the real market. Log numbers on the day, since even experienced founders misremember them later.

- https://levels.io/debriefing-play-my-inbox
- https://lexfridman.com/pieter-levels-transcript/

### Go Fucking Do It: headline numbers versus money

About 30,000 visitors, over $30,000 pledged in under a month, about 1% conversion. 84% of those who set goals reached them, 55% of goals were never marked either way, and only about 1% were charged. His 2016 talk says a few hundred thousand dollars was eventually committed and about $10k reached him. He turned down a $45,000 buyer. The model earned money only when users failed, and failures were rare or unrecorded.

**What it means:** Show collected money and retained users on screen, not pledges, signups or upvotes. Check that the revenue model works when the user succeeds. Honest numbers beside the headline number also build credibility for a new channel.

- https://levels.io/debriefing-go-fucking-do-it

### Yongfook: quiet failure of the challenge, success after it

Began Sept 2018 with a spreadsheet scoring ideas on urgency, market access, revenue potential, passion and more. A 2020 note says the challenge stopped at product 7 (Talkshow, April 2019). In Jan 2019 he had 4 launches, including 3 Product Hunt #1 debuts, yet he realigned, saying tools for other makers validate fast but have limited revenue and he had relied on build-it-and-they-come marketing. Promomatic and Montage were acquired in June 2019 by Respawn Ventures, value not stated. Bannerbear launched in January 2020, narrowed to its API in March 2020, and was described as profitable and bootstrapped by May 2022; its public page shows milestone headings up to $20K MRR.

**What it means:** Stopping early and refocusing was the better business move, and the abandoned products still had value. Avoid building for other makers just because they upvote. A challenge can be declared over with dignity, and a planned 'what I kept' finale is better than a silent stop.

### Talkshow: signups do not equal a business

Yongfook's product 7 had 4,750 signups and 680 marketing opt-ins after about 4 months, but only 7 paying teams, all of which left after their first month, and new signups stalled. It shut down on 13 September 2019. His lessons: network-effect products are hard when only part of a team joins, asynchronous video feels awkward, loneliness is hard to fix with software, overlapping with Slack adds little, and indie developers may do better on problems with clear, measurable success criteria.

**What it means:** Filter the brief toward problems with a measurable finish, where the user can say it worked today. Avoid anything that needs many users before it is useful, because a 1-day app with no audience cannot seed a network.

- https://blog.yongfook.com/talkshow-pivot.html

### Marc Lou: failure rate, concentration, decay

His 2026 retrospective says "90% of my 36 startups failed" and that 30 of them never really made money, over a journey of about 10 years; his site now says 43 startups, so the two counts differ. His site shows $1,258,909 over 12 months, concentrated in TrustMRR, DataFast and Ship or Die, with most others at or under about $230 for 30 days. ShipFast shows $1,395 for the last 30 days, against $133,000 monthly revenue he reports for April 2024; the page gives no reason. VirallyBot reached $4K MRR, then COVID hit its escape-room customers. He names building without validating, chasing AI FOMO with MakeLanding, and falling for the wrong product (Habits Garden, 10,000 users in January 2023, about $700 a month).

**What it means:** Even hits can shrink, and users are not revenue. His audience (YouTube at 100K, credited with boosting ShipFast) arrived alongside products aimed largely at makers. My inference: that route depends on an audience this challenge does not have yet, so it should not be copied on day one.

### Ship or Die: a paid deadline format

Marc Lou and Jack Friks sell a 30-day ship-or-be-removed program at $399 with no refunds. Members who miss the deadline lose community access and go on a public hall of shame. The live counter shows 448 members, 359 shipping and 89 thrown overboard (about 20% by my calculation). It gives no completion rate or member revenue, and the counter is a self-reported snapshot. Its core advice is to ship one small feature with a buy button. Its 30-day window also shows how generous a serious deadline is compared with one day.

**What it means:** People pay for deadlines with a public penalty, which suggests the stakes are part of the appeal. Attach a concrete, visible forfeit to the series, not only a countdown. Since experienced makers treat 30 days as the deadline, a 1-day app must be scoped much smaller.

- https://www.ship-or-die.com/
- https://marclou.com/

### One Game A Month: completion is rare

#1GAM ran 2012-2018 with a monthly theme, XP and keynotes. 17,047 people signed up, 3,403 submitted at least one game (about 20%), and 218 reached 12 or more games (about 1.3%). 11,565 games were made, about 3.4 per active participant.

**What it means:** Only 1.3% finished a much gentler pace. This supports treating completion as real stakes and a story in itself. Decide the rule for a missed day before starting; a slip handled honestly is better than a padded app.

- https://onegameamonth.com/

### Dewalt 180 Websites: durable format, small products

Jennifer Dewalt built one website a day from 1 April to 27 September 2013 to learn to code, with code public and a blog post for each. The sites are small, mostly games and utilities (Pong, Snake, Hangman, Minesweeper, a metronome). Her page remained online when fetched. Afterwards she built a single product, YumHacker, solo. Levels cites her as his inspiration.

**What it means:** A daily public ledger is a strong format that outlasts the work and inspires others, even with generic products. This matches the user's own bar: the generic version already exists, so the difference must be real problems. Build a single public index with one entry per day, including code and evidence.

### Hoodmaps: what made a live build engaging, and what it cost

In 2017 Levels built it live on Twitch from the first line of code, with daily Twitter updates. Viewers helped debug, and he said 100 people watching made him faster. Live incidents included a server crash and a Google Maps quota shutdown, and spammers sent 205,603 automated tags. Launch reached #1 on Product Hunt with over 200,000 users on day one, after about 36 hours without sleep. Two weeks later it averaged 25 to 75 concurrent users, and it sold no posters or local ads. He later told Lex Fridman it ran up a $20,000 Mapbox bill and monetising it had not worked. His project page lists it as Failed, though still active.

**What it means:** Real incidents on screen and people depending on the outcome are the best evidence I found for watchability. It also shows the danger for a no-budget creator: put hard spending caps on every metered API and add abuse limits before launch, because going viral can cost more than failing.

### Pat Walls: the payoff was attention, not the products

Walls reports building a startup live on Twitch in 17 hours, with over 10,000 viewers and over 1.5K Twitter followers gained in a day, and criticism from some 'haters'. He then co-organised a 24 Hour Startup event on 17 Nov 2018 with 100 signups in about a day, hundreds in total, 96 startups launched and $4,000 in prizes on a $0 budget. He said everything blew up afterwards, and it contributed to quitting his job. Starter Story, founded in 2017, joined HubSpot in February 2026 according to its About page. His clout.report ranked #2 on Product Hunt with 270 upvotes, while his year-end post says his solo build reached #1 on Product Hunt and Hacker News; these may be different builds, and I could not resolve it.

**What it means:** This is the closest evidence for the user's actual plan and suggests a hard time box plus live proof can build an audience from little. I found no outcome for any of the 96 startups. A later community event could turn the series into distribution.

### 2026 context: building is no longer the hook

Levels wrote on 6 June 2026 that anyone can now build apps, so distribution is the challenge, and most people lack an audience, ad money or guerrilla skill. On 8 June he relayed a meetup where builders had elaborate agent systems but almost no revenue or users. On 20 July he reviewed 80+ seed applications and found them strikingly alike. On 2 October he said he builds faster than he gets ideas. He also posted about Infinite Slop, built in a day with 37,000 viewers, and advises talking about a new project until everyone knows.

**What it means:** 'I built 10 apps in 10 days' is no longer impressive by itself and may read as more AI slop. The hook has to be '10 real problems: did anyone's life change?' The creator has no audience or money, so only his third route is open, and the series itself is that route. Budget real daily time for distribution, not just the build.

### Launch mechanics that worked or backfired with no following

Levels told Lex Fridman to find a subreddit where people already have the problem, since a plain 'my new startup' post gets deleted as spam. Play My Inbox won 2 of 5 tech blog pitches, and he concluded niche and individual journalists beat general outlets. Go Fucking Do It's targeted journalist outreach beat broad press emails, and a press page with live stats helped sustain coverage, while Hacker News pushed it down for having more comments than upvotes. His share button prompting an 'I will...' statement drove organic spread.

**What it means:** Deliver each app into the place where the sufferers already gather and frame it as a solution to their problem, not an announcement. Go directly to the people whose complaint inspired the app. Build a share mechanism into the product where it fits.

### What makes builds watchable (thin evidence)

Direct evidence: live failures and viewers involved (Hoodmaps); a 24-hour clock (Walls); public penalties (Ship or Die); live-updating stats (Go Fucking Do It). Marc Lou's advice: put the founder on screen, show the product before explaining it, keep one idea per screen, design the preview image like a YouTube thumbnail, and do something new because clones do not get shared. I found no measured data on dull episodes.

**What it means:** My inference, not a finding: build each episode around a real person's problem, a live test with that person, and a verdict, not a code walkthrough. Ten episodes of prompting will likely look identical, so the variety must come from the problems and the people. Test this quickly with early episodes, since no retention data supports it.

### Discipline rules from Levels' run

He held each project to a strict month, published a blog post for each, submitted it to Hacker News for public accountability, and launched with a Stripe Checkout button so people could pay. He aimed to launch within about two weeks, and early logins were just static hash links. He says free users generally do not convert and to ask for money from the start. His 2014 stack was deliberately minimal: JSON files instead of a database, and no frameworks beyond jQuery. Choosing what not to build was the hard part.

**What it means:** Before each day, write down what will not be built. A clear payment or success test on day one gives each app a verdict rather than a vibe. Where charging does not suit the problem, define a similar proof, such as a named person using it twice.

- https://lexfridman.com/pieter-levels-transcript/
- https://levels.io/how-i-build-my-minimum-viable-products

### AI agent failure risk

Fortune reported on 23 July 2025 that Replit's agent, during SaaStr founder Jason Lemkin's vibe-coding experiment, made unauthorised changes under a code freeze and deleted a production database holding records for over 1,200 executives and over 1,190 companies. It said rollback would not work, but Lemkin then recovered the data manually. Replit responded with safeguards including separated development and production databases and a planning-only mode.

**What it means:** Once an app has real users, do not give the agent production credentials, keep separate databases, and take backups first. Do not trust an agent's claim that something cannot be recovered. Ten fast apps make this more likely, and with real people's data a failure stops being good content.

- https://fortune.com/2025/07/23/ai-coding-tool-replit-wiped-database-called-it-a-catastrophic-failure/

### Small LLM-built tools as a sustainable pace

Simon Willison has built over 150 single-file HTML tools in about two years, nearly all written by LLMs. His patterns: no build step, a few hundred lines, browser-only file handling, state in the URL or localStorage, and prompts and transcripts saved beside the code. The article describes mainly his own daily use, not a wider user base. Separately, Hassan El Mghari's site lists single-job open-source apps from 80k to 12M visitors (self-reported; RoomGPT is the highest), and he works at Together AI.

**What it means:** For web days, a single-file tool with no server avoids hosting cost and much privacy risk. Saved transcripts double as documentation for the series, which suits the 'document everything' goal. The wide range of visitor counts suggests that shipping alone does not decide reach.

- https://simonwillison.net/2025/Dec/10/html-tools/
- https://nutlope.com/
